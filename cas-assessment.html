The CAS Assessment: A Guide for Families

Every adult who receives OPWDD services has a CAS assessment, usually every two years. It shapes service planning, so it's worth understanding.

Last reviewed November 10, 2026

What the CAS is

The Coordinated Assessment System (CAS) is the assessment OPWDD uses to understand a person's strengths, needs, and interests. It was developed by interRAI, an international research network, and OPWDD uses it because it's required to do a conflict-free needs assessment for people receiving its services.

The CAS is required for everyone 18 or older who receives or applies for OPWDD services. Children are assessed with a different tool, the CANS-NY (Child and Adolescent Needs and Strengths).

When it happens

  • After eligibility: an initial CAS follows the OPWDD eligibility determination.
  • Every two years: routine reassessments.
  • Sooner, after a big change: if a Care Manager determines there's been a Significant Change in Condition, such as a serious illness, a loss of skills, or a major change in behavior, the person is referred for reassessment.

How it works

A trained CAS assessor has a conversation with the person. If the person chooses, family members or others who know them well can take part. The assessor also reviews supporting documents, such as medical evaluations. OPWDD staff sometimes observe assessments for quality, but they don't take part in the interview.

What happens with the results

  1. A summary goes into the record

    Within 48 hours, a CAS Summary is added to the person's record in OPWDD's CHOICES system.

  2. The planning team reviews it

    Within 30 days, the Care Manager (or QIDP, for people in certain residential settings) reviews the summary with the person and their care planning team.

  3. It shapes the plan

    The team uses it in building the Life Plan. OPWDD also uses CAS data to set "service ranges" that guide care planning and service authorization.

How to prepare

  • Think about a typical week, not a good day. Note where the person needs help, prompting, or supervision.
  • Write down recent changes: new diagnoses, hospital stays, behaviors, skills gained or lost.
  • Gather recent records the assessor may want to see.
  • Decide with the person who should be in the room. Someone who knows daily routines well is most helpful.
  • After the assessment, ask your Care Manager for the CAS Summary and read it.

If you disagree with the results

Talk with your Care Manager or QIDP first; many issues get cleared up there. If questions remain, you can submit OPWDD's CAS/CANS Request for Review form, or call OPWDD at 518-473-7484.

For families who self-direct

Your Support Broker builds the Self-Direction budget from the Life Plan and the services the person is authorized for, so an assessment that reflects real needs makes the rest of the process smoother. If a CAS reassessment is coming up, tell your broker.
