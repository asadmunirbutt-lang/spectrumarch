If someone is missing, call 911 immediately. Don't wait to search on your own first. Tell the dispatcher the person has a disability and may be drawn to water or roads.

Many families of children and adults with autism or intellectual disabilities know the fear of a door left open for a moment. Wandering, also called elopement, is common, and it can be dangerous, especially near water or traffic. Planning ahead lowers the risk.

Understand the why

People usually wander toward something (water, a favorite place, an interesting object) or away from something, such as noise, crowds, or stress. Noticing patterns helps you prevent it: when does it happen, where do they go, what came just before?

Secure the home

  • Add locks or latches placed high on doors, and door or window alarms or chimes.
  • Fence the yard, and pay special attention to pools and nearby water.
  • Use visual "stop" signs on doors as a reminder for people who respond to them.

Check fire safety: everyone else in the house must still be able to get out quickly.

Identification

  • An ID bracelet, shoe tag, or clothing label with a phone number
  • A GPS tracking device, if the person will tolerate it
  • An up-to-date photo on your phone

Some sheriff's offices run locating programs, such as Project Lifesaver, for people at risk of wandering. Ask your county whether one is available.

Tell the people around you

  • Give neighbors a photo, your phone number, and what to do if they see your family member alone.
  • Consider letting local police know in advance, so they have information if they're ever called.
  • Make sure schools, programs, and staff have a wandering plan too.

Teach safety skills

Where possible, practice responding to their name, stopping on request, and carrying or showing an ID card. Swimming lessons can be life-saving for people drawn to water.

Sources: Ready.gov, People with Disabilities; Project Lifesaver International.
