The short answer: an OPWDD Support Broker helps a person with a developmental disability who self-directs their services build and run their plan. The broker's main job is to develop and maintain the Self-Direction budget, working with the person and their Circle of Support. Brokers also help hire staff, track spending, keep paperwork in order, and make sure the plan keeps meeting the person's needs.

Whether you're looking for a Support Broker for yourself or for a son, daughter, or other family member, this is what you can expect. Everything below comes from OPWDD's Support Broker Agreement (June 2026) and its Self-Direction Support Broker Checklist.

The five duties every Support Broker must provide

Each broker signs a Support Broker Agreement with the person who self-directs. OPWDD's form lists five services that must be delivered:

  1. Building and running the Circle of Support. Helping the person choose members, assign roles, schedule and lead meetings, and making sure members are freely chosen by the person and act in their best interest.
  2. Meeting with the Circle of Support at least twice a year.
  3. Keeping Staff Action Plans current for self-hired Community Habilitation and Supported Employment staff, if the person uses those services. Staff Action Plans are reviewed at least twice a year.
  4. Developing a Self-Direction budget that's consistent with the person's Life Plan.
  5. Making sure needed safeguards are in the Life Plan, working with the person and their Circle of Support.

Other duties you can agree on

The agreement also lists services the broker and the person can choose to include. Only the services written into the agreement can be paid, so it's worth deciding together what you need:

  • Helping develop a comprehensive Life Plan
  • Teaching the person and Circle of Support how to use the budget within Medicaid and OPWDD rules
  • Finding community connections and resources that support the person's goals, with the Care Manager
  • Tracking spending against the approved budget by reviewing the Fiscal Intermediary's monthly expenditure report
  • Reviewing and updating the budget as needs change, so it stays current and eligible for Medicaid funding
  • Attending Life Plan reviews
  • Making sure at least two face-to-face Circle of Support meetings happen each year
  • Paperwork: helping review and submit timesheets, monthly summary notes, mileage and expense forms to the Fiscal Intermediary
  • Hiring and keeping staff: help with recruiting, interviewing, hiring, scheduling, and supervising self-hired staff, and lining up backup staff

OPWDD's checklist adds that brokers should look for good, cost-effective goods and services, check other funding sources before using the budget, stay current on OPWDD rules, and keep in regular contact with the person's Care Manager, Fiscal Intermediary, and the regional office's Self-Direction Liaison.

What a Support Broker doesn't do

  • They're not the employer. The person or family hires and supervises staff; the Fiscal Intermediary is the employer of record and runs payroll.
  • They don't replace the Care Manager. The Care Manager writes the Life Plan, coordinates services, and helps keep Medicaid. The broker focuses on the Self-Direction budget and plan.
  • They don't decide for the person. Self-Direction is built on the person's own choices. The broker supports those choices.

Questions people ask about Support Brokers

Do I need a Support Broker to self-direct?

If you self-direct with Budget Authority, meaning you decide how the Self-Direction budget is spent, yes. OPWDD describes Support Brokers as helping people who self-direct with Budget Authority develop a Circle of Support and complete and manage their budget.

How often does a Support Broker meet with you?

The Circle of Support must meet at least twice a year, and OPWDD expects at least two of those meetings each year to be face to face. The broker and the person must meet in person unless a virtual meeting has been pre-approved and documented in the Life Plan. Many families meet more often, especially while the first budget is being built.

Who pays the Support Broker?

Support Brokerage is an OPWDD waiver service funded through Medicaid, so families don't usually pay out of pocket. The broker's hours and hourly fee are written into the Support Broker Agreement and can't exceed what's in the approved Self-Direction budget. Brokerage during the start-up phase, before the first budget is approved, is funded separately.

What's the difference between a Support Broker and a Care Manager?

The Care Manager writes and updates the Life Plan, coordinates all services, and helps the person keep Medicaid. The Support Broker focuses on Self-Direction: building and managing the budget, running the Circle of Support, and helping with staff and paperwork. The Care Manager must be invited to Circle of Support meetings, so the two work together.

Can a family member be my Support Broker?

Yes, but parents, legal guardians, spouses, adult children (including in-laws), and anyone in the same household can't be paid for it. Family members can serve as unpaid brokers after completing OPWDD's initial broker training. See Can a parent be a Support Broker?

Can I have more than one Support Broker?

Yes. More than one broker can assist a person, but one must be named as the primary broker, and each one needs their own signed Support Broker Agreement.

Can I change my Support Broker?

Yes. You choose your broker, and you can change if the fit isn't right. The current agreement is ended with a termination form, and a new agreement is signed with the new broker.

How do I know a Support Broker is qualified?

Paid brokers must complete OPWDD's training, be authorized by OPWDD, and reauthorize every year by July 31, including 12 hours of professional development. Ask for their authorization number. See how to check a broker is authorized.

How do I find a Support Broker near me?

Ask your Care Manager, check OPWDD's public list of authorized brokers, or contact a local organization.
