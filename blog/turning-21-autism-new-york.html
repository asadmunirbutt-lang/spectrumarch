Turning 21 With Autism in New York: What Ends, What Doesn't, and What to Do First

By Asad M. Butt, President, Spectrum Arch, Inc. · September 23, 2026 · 3 min read

TransitionTurning 21OPWDD

Families call it "the cliff." For years, school gives your son or daughter a schedule, a team, transportation, and people who know them. Then, at the end of the school year in which they turn 21, it stops. What replaces it is not automatic. It has to be applied for, planned, and sometimes waited for.

The good news is that most of what you need to do can be done early, and the families who start early have far more choices. Here is what actually changes, and what to do at each age.

What ends at 21

In New York, a student with an IEP is entitled to special education, including transition services, until they earn a Regents or local diploma or until the end of the school year in which they turn 21, whichever comes first. After that, the school district has no further responsibility. That means:

  • No more school day, school bus, or school-based therapies
  • No more Committee on Special Education (CSE) meetings
  • No more IEP. Adult services use different plans, run by different people

What doesn't end

Some things carry straight through, as long as they are in place:

  • OPWDD eligibility. If your child has been found eligible for OPWDD services, that eligibility continues into adulthood.
  • Medicaid and the HCBS Waiver, which pay for most adult OPWDD services, including day programs, community habilitation, respite, and residential homes.
  • Care management. Your Care Manager keeps coordinating services and updating the Life Plan.

The catch: these have to be set up before school ends. Families who apply for OPWDD eligibility at 20 often spend the first months after graduation with nothing in place.

A year-by-year checklist

Ages 15 to 17: planning starts in the IEP

  • New York requires transition planning in the IEP beginning no later than the school year a student turns 15. Use those goals. Ask what the school is doing about daily living skills, work experience, and community travel.
  • If your child is not yet OPWDD-eligible, start that application now. Eligibility requires records showing a developmental disability that began before age 22, and gathering evaluations takes time.
  • Ask the school about a referral to ACCES-VR, New York's vocational rehabilitation service, if work is a goal.

Age 18: adulthood, legally

  • At 18, your child is a legal adult. Decide how decisions will be made. New York recognizes Supported Decision-Making agreements, which let an adult choose trusted supporters while keeping their own rights. Guardianship is another option. Talk with an attorney or an advocate about which fits.
  • Look into SSI. At 18, Social Security looks at the young adult's own income and resources, not the parents'.
  • Make sure Medicaid is in place, since OPWDD's adult services depend on it.

Ages 19 and 20: choose what comes next

  • Visit day programs, and ask about waitlists.
  • Learn about Self-Direction, where your family chooses supports and hires staff with a budget. Our step-by-step Self-Direction guide explains how it works.
  • If you are thinking about a residential home, tell your Care Manager now. Residential openings are limited in the Capital District, and waits can run well over a year.

The final school year

  • Confirm start dates for every adult service, so there is no gap in the summer after graduation.
  • Get copies of the last IEP, evaluations, and transition assessments. Adult providers will ask for them.
  • Plan the first weeks after school ends. Routine matters, and a sudden empty schedule is hard for many autistic adults.

If you are already past 21

It is not too late. Start with OPWDD eligibility, if that isn't done, and a Care Manager. From there, the same options open up; they just take a little longer to put in place.

Sources: NYSED, Transition Planning and Services for Students with Disabilities; ACCES-VR Youth in School Transition Policy; NY Mental Hygiene Law Article 82 (Supported Decision-Making); OPWDD.
